Project 03 / Design Thinking
Project 03 / Design Thinking London · Dec 2024 - Feb 2025

Sound as Structure.

A design-thinking study of how sound shapes a learning space. This project involved measuring the acoustic life of my college workshop block and translating that invisible data into a tactile, foldable infographic that literally lets the reader hear the building.

01
The Question

The brief asked me to communicate complexity. I chose to make sound visible.

The brief was intentionally open-ended: take something complex and communicate it visually. While wandering the workshop block with no fixed focus, a constant, sharp beeping near the sound studios made me hyper-aware of noises I would normally tune out.

That one sound reframed the entire space. I realised noise wasn't just a neutral background element; it was actively steering how the rooms felt and how people behaved. I narrowed my focus from the entire physical building down to a single, slippery design material: sound as an architectural layer.

How does sound influence learning environments, and how can its role be visually represented to highlight its impact on focus and collaboration?

02
Method

I walked every room I could get into and listened.

A decibel meter can tell you how loud a room is, but it can't tell you how the room feels. I started by walking every room I could get into just to listen and observe.

Louder rooms swarmed with energy — machinery whirring, voices overlapping — producing a sense of urgency, but also deep distraction. Quieter rooms felt almost motionless and lonely: easy to focus, but completely drained of collaborative momentum.

The contrast became the primary insight. Sound wasn't a passive byproduct of the space; it was actively dictating concentration. Two rooms just a few metres apart were asking completely different things of the exact same student.

Initial observation notes - the first time "acoustics" got circled as the theme.

03
The Field

The second floor held the sharpest contrast, so it became the subject.

I chose the second floor of the workshop block because it held the sharpest, most honest architectural contrast in the building along a single corridor.

Rather than averaging out the data of the whole building, I leaned directly into this corridor's unique tension. The space houses polar opposites simultaneously: the lively, communal buzz of the print, finishing, and letterpress rooms sits immediately adjacent to the quiet, isolated sanctuary of the W-rooms and the kit room.

Floor 2 - from the busy kit room to the near-silent W-rooms.

04
What the Data Showed

I measured each zone with sound apps, across different times of day.

I sampled each zone repeatedly between December and January using Decibel X (for decibel levels and graph readings) and Sonic Tools (to capture the frequency spectrum) to map exactly how the building's acoustic activity rose and fell.

The surprise in the data came during class transitions. I assumed noise would peak when students flooded the halls between sessions. Instead, noise dropped drastically. The loudest hours were actually caused by sustained, collaborative group work inside the studios, not the brief rush of students moving between classes.

05
Secondary Research

The numbers only meant something against published guidelines.

To turn these numbers into actionable insights, I measured them against official UK acoustic-design standards and student realities. UK guidelines state that standard classrooms shouldn't exceed 35 dB, while high-activity workshops are expected to sit between 45–65 dB. Research shows that moderate ambient noise (50–60 dB) can actually boost creative thinking, but anything past 65 dB completely breaks down a student's ability to concentrate.

80%
of students report experiencing disruptive noise levels in educational spaces — highlighting a massive gap between architectural guidelines and daily reality.

To cross-examine the literature with real-world preferences, I ran a quick survey on Reddit. The responses split cleanly down the middle: focus-seekers demanded absolute silence or low ambient sound, while collaborators actively sought out moderate background noise to spark energy. Both needs were completely valid, yet entirely opposed.

06
The Tension

One building, two opposite acoustic needs at the same time.

The research pointed to a single, systemic challenge: the floor has to serve intense individual focus and high-energy collaboration at the exact same time. Sound is the invisible lever that tips a space toward one or the other.

Foundation

Quiet zones

Low, steady sound. The necessary base layer that lets individual concentration happen — the W-rooms.

Dynamic layer

Collaborative zones

Higher, livelier frequencies. The energy layer that drives peer exchange and creative momentum — print and letterpress.

The friction doesn't happen because the print shop is loud; it happens when noise spills from a collaborative zone into a quiet one, breaking the functionality of both. The goal wasn't to silence the building, it was to visually map sound as a structural layer so users could navigate it effectively.

07
The Artifact

I built the data into a poster you have to open with your hands.

I translated this spatial data into a physical, interactive poster that the reader has to unpack and explore with their hands. Modelled after the physical behaviour of sound waves, the layout stacks quiet foundations underneath the more chaotic, collaborative layers.

A Spatial Map tracking decibel intensity across the physical blueprint of the second floor, supported by a 3D model built in Arcadium 3D. A Sankey flow tracing exactly where the sound originates — 47.6% humans, 42.1% machines, 10.3% environmental ambient noise. Tactile Flaps — physical paper folds that force the reader to manually lift and uncover the specific acoustic profile of each zone.

Ideation

Sketches and paper folds - figuring out the artefact.

Artefact

The finished folded artefact - an interactive foldable poster

Before finalising the print, I added an interactive graph directly onto the artefact that invites readers to plot their own preferred sound levels. This small addition shifts the viewer from a passive audience member into an active data point, closing the loop on a project dedicated to human perception.

08
Reflection

The hard part wasn't collecting data. It was knowing what to cut.

The hardest part of this project wasn't collecting the data; it was knowing what to cut. When you are measuring something completely invisible, there is an overwhelming temptation to display every single piece of evidence to prove your rigour. My first design draft buried the core narrative under 25 near-identical smartphone screenshots of decibel meters.

The project only became successful when I learned to trust a handful of highly contrasting readings to carry the weight of the story. I realised that design judgment means separating raw data from user attention — giving the viewer the space to feel the acoustic contrast rather than exhausting them with proof.

What I'd push further
If I had more time, I would take this static paper project and move it onto a live-updating digital screen driven by real-time decibel sensors in the hallways. Testing that digital artefact directly with the students on the second floor would allow the very people being measured to read their own acoustic behaviour back in real time.
