01 Context

The enquiry, with no outcome in mind.

The brief asked us to take an everyday interaction and reimagine it through making - using making itself as a method of enquiry rather than a route to a fixed answer. The approach drew on Schön’s idea of reflection-in-action: decisions made by engaging with materials and people, not by planning in advance.

We started with no outcome in mind. We went into Selfridges and watched how people actually move through the beauty floor, grouping what we saw into three kinds of interaction - human, tech, and sensory - and researching each one to understand what made it work or fail.

02 Findings

What the observation surfaced.

Documenting the floor through self-ethnography and observation surfaced three recurring frictions: product information that was hard to decode, fragile displays that made people anxious to touch, and an overwhelming amount of choice that stalled decisions.

03 The pivot

Getting lost.

The turn was almost accidental. We went to try Click & Collect - an interaction we’d already been circling - and couldn’t find it. A staff member sent us to the first floor; there was no signage or map; we asked again and were finally pointed to the lower ground floor.

This simple confusion became our biggest insight.

That moment of getting lost, not any product, became the biggest insight. Customer experience reaches beyond the products on a counter into spatial navigation and the guidance people rely on to move through a space.

04 Ideation

Keeping options open.

Rather than narrow too early, we mapped every friction we’d found in a shared board - unclear product language, fragile displays, overwhelming choice, confusing layouts - and deliberately kept our options open to see which problem sparked the most energy before committing. My own contribution centred on the self-ethnographic research and several of the early interaction ideas.

Chosen ↓ How might we design navigation and wayfinding for clearer, more inclusive movement through a space?
Explored How might we improve the clarity of product information?
Explored How might we make interaction with fragile displays feel safe?
05 First prototype

What didn’t work.

Our first prototype, Guess Who, tried to tackle the beauty language barrier by turning product-vs-customer vocabulary into a yes/no guessing game between shopper and advisor.

Testing showed the opposite of what we wanted: the rules added cognitive load instead of reducing it. The failure was productive - it told us simplicity and familiarity mattered more than novelty, and our tutor pointed out that navigation offered far more room for enquiry.

What the failure taught us

Clarity for the designer isn’t clarity for the user.

06 Direction

Committing to navigation.

Navigation pulled together the Click & Collect insight and the tutor’s steer. We took inspiration from the tactile guiding paths used by blind and low-vision people and adapted them for everyone: a continuous navigation line that uses pattern, texture, and colour, with a unique pattern per destination.

To test the existing baseline, we ran an observational study on the third floor of the Design Block, asking a user to find the toilet using current signage. She took several confusing turns, couldn’t read the route, and eventually gave up.

07 Audit

Testing the line.

We taped a low-fidelity navigation line along the walls and around doorways and tested it with the same user. She moved faster and more confidently, with noticeably less hesitation. Her suggestion - that the line should continue across the floor at a doorway and match the door’s colour - fed straight into the next iteration.

08 Making

Designing for each destination.

An inspiration board pulled together sensory and interaction-based references, and early testing asked a blunt question: could material, shape, and sound explain a destination without any text? Users leaned on touch and sound to make sense of the objects, and small material changes made a big difference to how intentional each one felt. From there we designed destination by destination.

WC

Blue reads as water

Rotating blue-and-white balls; turned up blue they read as water, and water reads as the toilets. Grew out of an earlier water-flow puzzle.

Library

Books you read in order

Stacked acrylic-and-wood “books” with embossed letters and laser-cut directional arrows, read in alphabetical order.

Café

Beans that rustle

3D-printed coffee beans filled with lentils so they rustle like a real café when rotated.

Lift

Follow the light

Arduino push-buttons with sequential lights; press one and the next lights up, so you “follow the light.”

I worked out the box dimensions for the build; my teammate, who codes, solved the lift’s interaction flow.

09 Fabrication

Building it.

A cardboard prototype let us test the whole system at presentation, but the material was unstable - wiring worked loose, tape failed, and elements detached under pressure. It made the lesson concrete: material choice drives usability, durability, and user confidence. Tutors found it fun and interactive, loved the rustling coffee beans, and pushed us to build it properly.

For the final build we learned laser cutting and rebuilt the design in Illustrator. The laser’s slight offset meant the box joints had to be corrected by hand, and I worked out the box dimensions on my own. We drilled a channel for the wiring and re-soldered the connections that kept coming loose. A laser-cut tactile floor-map poster - ual: LCC Tactile Wayfinding - replaced a digital one to support the board.

10 Outcome

The outcome.

The final mockup turns the concept into a tactile, material-based wayfinding system, experienced through touch rather than read off a screen. Sturdier construction resolved the earlier instability, and users navigate by following physical cues - making navigation an experience rather than a purely visual task.

Final testing was revealing. Users found the WC interaction fun and engaging; one user found the café’s sensory elements overwhelming and noted the system could be hard for people with autism, suggesting text alongside the icons. That single insight reframed the whole project around inclusivity.

11 Reflection

What making taught me.

Confusion and material failure stopped being mistakes - they became the most useful data I had.

Making as enquiry turned out to be a reflective, knowledge-generating process rather than a straight line to a solution.

The autism insight brought the project full circle, back to the tactile guiding paths that first inspired it. If I took it further, I’d focus on tactile and sensory navigation tuned to different sensory intensities and needs, so the system is genuinely responsive to the people using it.
